\reportsection{2. RANSAC Line and Circle Fitting}

\begin{wrapfigure}{r}{0.48\textwidth}
    
    \vspace{-22pt}
    \includegraphics[width=0.46\textwidth]
    {../outputs/q2_ransac_line_circle.png}
    %\caption{RANSAC line and circle fitting.}
    \label{fig:q2-ransac}
    \vspace{-10pt}
\end{wrapfigure}

RANSAC was implemented to estimate a line first, followed by a circle fitted to the points remaining after removing the line inliers. The line inlier threshold was 1.4, and the circle radial-error threshold was 1.2.

The estimated line parameters were
\[
a=0.716670,\quad b=0.697413,\quad d=1.502897.
\]
Therefore, the fitted line is
\[
0.716670x+0.697413y=1.502897.
\]
The unit-normal constraint was satisfied: $\sqrt{a^2+b^2}=1.000000$. The final line contained 54 inliers out of 100 points.

The estimated circle centre and radius were
\[
(c_x,c_y)=(1.969390,\;2.942512),\qquad r=9.907175.
\]
The circle contained 44 inliers. These estimates are close to the ground-truth centre $(2,3)$ and radius $10$.

The plot shows the best sample models, refined models, ground truth models, line/circle inliers, and the 3-point minimal sample for circle estimation. Some points may be assigned to the wrong model due to noise and spatial overlap.

\reportsubsection{What if the circle were fitted first?}

Fitting the circle first is significantly more sensitive to noise and outliers. Because circles possess 3 degrees of freedom (centre $x,y$ and radius $r$), sampling 3 points from a combined line-and-circle point cloud often forms spurious candidate circles whose arcs align locally with the straight line or tangent points. This leads to false consensus consensus subsets containing line points. Fitting the 2-DOF line first ($ax+by=d$) isolates the dominant linear structure reliably before fitting the 3-DOF circle to residual points.

\reportsubsection{Important implementation details}

The line RANSAC loop samples two points and counts inliers using perpendicular distance. The circle RANSAC loop samples three points and counts inliers using radial error. The best models are refined using total least squares for the line and squared radial-error minimization for the circle.

\begin{lstlisting}
# Line inlier test
line_error = np.abs(X @ np.array([a, b]) - d)
line_inliers = line_error < 1.4

# Circle inlier test
radial_error = np.abs(
    np.linalg.norm(points - center, axis=1) - radius
)
circle_inliers = radial_error < 1.2
\end{lstlisting}

The British flag was projected onto a planar region of the architectural image using a homography transformation. Four corresponding corner points were selected manually in the background image, and the homography matrix was computed to map the flag image onto the selected region.

The estimated homography matrix was

\[
H =
\begin{bmatrix}
0.17895975 & 0.00881057 & 301.00000000\\
-0.01704851 & 0.20405100 & 276.00000000\\
-0.00003723 & 0.00000152 & 1.00000000

The flag image was warped using this matrix and composited with the original architectural image. Figure~\ref{fig:q3-composite} shows the resulting composite, while Figure~\ref{fig:q3-warped} shows the warped flag.

The results demonstrate that a homography can map an image onto a selected planar region in another image. The alignment depends on the accuracy of the selected corner points. Although the flag is successfully projected onto the building, the result does not reproduce the surface texture, lighting, or shadows of the architecture.

\textbf{Discussion}

The difference images show residual edges and component details after perspective alignment. These differences may result from small alignment errors, variations in component positions, changes in illumination, or differences between the PCB images. A high difference around a component may indicate that its appearance or position differs from the reference, although it does not by itself establish a physical defect.

All three images produced high RANSAC inlier ratios, indicating that reliable feature correspondences were available for estimating the transformations. However, the inlier ratio measures geometric agreement between matched planar features rather than the overall 3D pixel similarity of the aligned images.

The USB Type B port, metallic capacitors, and reset button exhibit elevated differences primarily due to 3D parallax effects and specular reflections. Because a 2D planar homography ($3 \times 3$ matrix) assumes all points lie on a single 2D plane, tall 3D components experience out-of-plane perspective distortion and height relief parallax when viewed from slightly different camera viewpoints. Additionally, shiny metallic housings (e.g., USB shell, capacitor tops) create strong specular highlights and shadow shifts under varying lighting directions. Therefore, a high pixel difference alone does not prove that a component is defective; it should be interpreted alongside the aligned image and the expected PCB layout.
